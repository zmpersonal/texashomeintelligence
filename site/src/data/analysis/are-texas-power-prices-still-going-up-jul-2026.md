## The short answer

**Yes.** Texas residential electricity is dearer than it was a year ago.

The average residential price in July 2026 was 15.88 cents per kilowatt-hour — up 3.4% year over year, and
down 6.5% from the peak (U.S. Energy Information Administration, as of July 2026).

## The series, not the headline

A single month's price is a number; the shape either side of it is the answer. Texas residential
power is measured here against its own preceding months and against the same month a year
earlier — never against another state, and never against a bill, which depends on how much you
used.

- **Against last year:** 15.88 cents per kilowatt-hour now, up 3.4% year over year.
- **Against this cycle:** down 6.5% from the peak.
- **The sharpest single move we hold:** a 6.4% rise in one month.

## "It was just a mild summer"

The obvious explanation is the weather: a mild July means less cooling, less demand, less strain. It is at least available this time — San Antonio ran below normal. Austin recorded 644 cooling degree-days in July 2026 (NOAA NCEI Global Summary of the Month, as of July 2026), against a July normal of 644.8 cooling degree-days (NOAA NCEI U.S. Climate Normals 1991-2020) — -0.1% against the normal. San Antonio recorded 607 cooling degree-days (NOAA NCEI Global Summary of the Month, as of July 2026) against 643.3 cooling degree-days (NOAA NCEI U.S. Climate Normals 1991-2020) — -5.6% against the normal. Available is not the same as established, and nothing we hold measures the link.

## What we are not saying

We cannot say from the data we hold what moved the price; the cause is not established here. A price series records what was charged. It does not record why, and the
explanations that sound most obvious — fuel costs, a rate change, contract mix — are exactly the
ones we hold no data for. So we are not going to tell you which it was.
